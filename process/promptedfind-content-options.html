3The Gap — lead + support
3A

Video is easy to watch and hard to search. A document has headings; a video only has time.

Prompted Find makes the timeline navigable by meaning. Ask what you want to find, and the answer is a timestamp you can trust, not a transcript or a summary.

Draft from the variant. Balanced, states the insight cleanly.

3B

You can skim a document. You cannot skim a 74-minute talk.

Headings give text an address; video only has a clock. Prompted Find gives the timeline addresses: ask what you're looking for, and the answer is a place in the video, not a wall of transcript.

Starts from the reader's own experience (“you”), uses the real 74-minute number, and “addresses” sets up the method section without naming it yet.

3C

Finding one moment in a long video still means scrubbing and guessing.

Prompted Find replaces the guess with a question. The timeline splits into labeled segments, your question highlights the relevant ones, and one click lands you there.

Most mechanical: says exactly what happens on screen. Least lyrical, most literal.

4The Method — lead + support the abstraction; keep it to two lines
4A

Semantic Decomposition: split anything long into labeled units, so a question can return a location.

Prompted Find is the method applied to video. The same move works on any surface with length but no structure: prose, podcasts, research trails.

Draft from the variant. Definition-first; fine but reads like a glossary entry.

4B

The method is Semantic Decomposition: give unstructured content a structure a question can point into.

Here it's applied to video. The same decomposition works on prose, podcasts, and research trails; only the surface changes.

Leads with “the method is…” so a scanner knows why this section exists; “point into” connects back to the timestamp idea.
